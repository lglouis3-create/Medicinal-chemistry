OBJECTIVE (his slide 4)Explain factors influencing DMslides 32–67

Read this first — the words on these slides

Intrinsic factor
An inside-the-body factor in drug metabolism (DM): a property of the parent drug (PD) or of the patient's physiology (slides 32–33).
Extrinsic factor
Exposure to xenobiotics from outside; the key considerations are induction- and inhibition-related drug–drug interactions (DDIs) (slides 51–52).
DME, DME co-factor
Drug-metabolising enzyme and the co-substrate it consumes (UDPGA, PAPS, acetyl-CoA, glutathione, SAM: slides 29–30); both are physiological factors (slides 33, 41–42).
Pre-systemic (first-pass) metabolism
Metabolism of an oral (PO) dose in the gut and on the first pass through the liver; it diminishes PD bioavailability (slide 35).
ABC transporters
ATP Binding Cassette, mostly efflux: P-gp or MDR1 (Multi-Drug Resistance) and MRPs (MRP1–4) (slide 37).
SLC transporters
Solute Carrier influx/efflux: OATs (Organic Anion Transporters) and OCTs (Organic Cation Transporters) (slide 37).
Serum protein binding
Weak acids bind albumin, weak bases bind α-1 acid glycoprotein; only the free (unbound) drug is metabolised (slide 38).
Tissue binding
Reversible or covalent, non-specific binding to tissue proteins or lipids; raises total (free + bound) tissue concentration (slide 39).
Polypharmacy
Many drugs at once; an age factor (slide 44) and an elderly factor (slide 47).
Sexual dimorphism
Male–female difference in the DM of some drugs (slide 48, a figure; slide 67: "Gender (Sex hormones)").
Genetic polymorphism
Inherited DME variation within and across species; CYP2D6 phenotypes: poor (PM), intermediate (IM), ultra-rapid (UM) and extensive (EM) metabolisers (slide 49).
Enzyme induction
↑ enzyme levels (↑ mRNA translation) and activity, ↓ protein degradation; net ↓ drug blood levels and ↓ DOA (duration of action) (slide 53).
Enzyme inhibition
Reversible (lasts 2–3 days) or irreversible (enzyme complexed, must be regenerated); net ↑ drug levels and DOA (slide 59). Mechanism-based (slide 63): (T) the enzyme itself makes the species that ties it up.
Tyramine
A dietary amine (smoked/aged/pickled meats, fish, sauerkraut, cheese) that releases epinephrine (Epi) and norepinephrine (Norepi); with a monoamine oxidase inhibitor (MAOI), hypertensive crisis (slide 61).

2. Dose (slide 34)
  • Overdosing can overwhelm DME capacity, leading to toxicity.
  • Example: APAP (acetaminophen).
  • (T) "you shut down those pathways. And now the less important pathways for metabolism become prominent," giving "electrophilic intermediates" and "fulminant hepatotoxicity."
  • (T) Daily limit "3 to 4 g".
3. Route (slide 35)
  • The oral (PO) route is susceptible to pre-systemic metabolism in (i) the gut (stomach, intestine mucosa and microbiome) and (ii) the first time the drug passes through the liver.
  • The overall impact is diminished PD bioavailability.

5. Absorption: transporters (slide 37)
  • ABC (mostly efflux): P-gp or MDR1, MRP1–4.
  • SLC influx/efflux: OATs, OCTs.
  • Induction or inhibition of transporters can affect the drug amounts reaching the liver for metabolism.
  • (T) "It doesn't matter whether these are P-gp or solute carrier ... they have a say in how much of these drugs get metabolised."

19. Inducer case: cyclosporin A (slide 55)
  • Cyclosporin A (CsA, an immunosuppressant used to prevent transplant rejection) is a substrate of 3A4.
  • Co-administration with 3A4 inducers can result in transplant failure.
  • Explain: the inducer raises 3A4 level and activity (slide 53).
  • More CsA is converted to inactive metabolites per unit time.
  • CsA blood level and DOA fall below the therapeutic range.
  • Immunosuppression is lost and the graft is rejected.
  • (T) "there's more of this metabolism happening ... inactivated cyclosporine. So, sub-therapy scenario is what happens here, transplant rejection."
  • Inducers to name: rifampin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, barbiturates, St Johns Wort (slide 54 figure).
20. Dietary and industrial inducers (slides 56–58)
  • Indoles from cruciferous vegetables (sprouts, cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower) ↑ CYP1A1 and 1A2 (slide 56).
  • Organosulfur compounds (e.g. garlic) ↑ CYP1A, CYP3A plus some phase II enzymes (slide 56).
  • PAHs (polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons; smoked/barbecued foods, cigarette smoke) ↑ CYP1A1 and 1A2 (slide 57).
  • Dioxin and polychlorinated biphenyl (PCB) pollutants ↑ multiple CYP and some UGT isoforms (slide 58).
21. Inhibitor case: tyramine + MAOIs = hypertensive crisis (slide 61)
  • Tyramine promotes the release of the hypertensive monoamines Epi and Norepi.
  • ↑ systemic tyramine, due to inhibition of its metabolic inactivation by MAOs, can precipitate a fatal hypertensive crisis.
  • (T) The "cheese crisis": "you want the monoamine oxidases to be left alone."
  • (T) He cited St. John's Wort, "over the counter," as an MAO inhibitor.
  • The printed lists (L13 slide 32, slide 54 figure) file it as an inducer, which is what an exam keys.
22. Dietary inhibitor: grapefruit juice (slides 62–64)
  • Grapefruit juice (GFJ) contains furocoumarins, which inhibit or ↓ CYP activity (slide 62).
  • Bergamottin and 6′,7′-dihydroxybergamottin are the most abundant furocoumarins in GFJ and are mechanism-based inhibitors of 3A4 (slide 63).
  • GFJ interferes with the metabolism of several drugs (slide 64, a list picture; the L13 slide 35 table marks felodipine "Y").
  • (T) The furocoumarin "double bond ... can undergo epoxidation ... 3A4 ... gets tied up with this. It forms an adduct ... this is called mechanism-based inhibition," "especially in the small intestine"; "a cup" is enough.

24. Q: the GFJ and felodipine plot (slide 66)
  • Felodipine's structure beside a "Single Dose Study" plot of plasma concentration (nmol/L) against hours, water versus grapefruit juice.
  • Read from the slide 66 plot: water peaks at about 2.5 nmol/L, GFJ at about 10–11 nmol/L, both at 2–3 h.
  • GFJ stays higher until both approach zero at 24 h.
  • So the time to peak is unchanged and only the height rises, about fourfold.
  • Explanation: felodipine is a GFJ-interacting 3A4 drug (L13 slide 35) taken by mouth.
  • With water most of the dose is lost to pre-systemic metabolism in the gut and first-pass liver (slide 35).
  • The furocoumarins are mechanism-based inhibitors of intestinal 3A4 (slide 63).
  • The pre-systemic loss is blocked.
  • Bioavailability, drug level and DOA rise as the slide 59 "Generally" line predicts.
  • (T) "you inhibit this pre-systemic metabolism in the gut, and you start seeing what? Higher levels of bioavailability increases."
{{fig:fa_gfj_felodipine|Slide 66 plot: single-dose felodipine plasma concentration with water (peak about 2.5 nmol/L) and with grapefruit juice (peak about 10 to 11 nmol/L at 2 to 3 h)}}
Memory hook From the "Generally" lines: induction sends parent-drug levels, DOA and efficacy ↓ (only prodrug effect and bioactivation toxicity go ↑); inhibition sends parent-drug levels, activity and DOA ↑ and metabolites ↓. Reversible inhibition has a time limit (2–3 days); irreversible lasts until new enzyme is made.

  • Inducer list vs inhibitor list: ritonavir and St Johns Wort are printed as inducers (slide 54 figure); azoles, macrolides, cimetidine, quinidine, omeprazole are inhibitors (slide 60 figure).
  • Hyperthyroidism vs the other disease rows: the one row that raises DME activity (slide 43).

  • Step 1 — place the interacting agent on one list: inducer (slide 54 figure, slides 56–58) or inhibitor (slide 60 figure, GFJ, MAOI).
  • Then decide whether the affected drug is an active parent drug or a prodrug.
  • Step 2 — apply the "Generally" line: inducer + active drug = ↓ levels, ↓ DOA, ↓ efficacy (cyclosporin: transplant failure).
  • Inhibitor + active drug = ↑ levels, ↑ DOA, exaggerated action (glyburide: hypoglycemia; Coumadin: hemorrhage; dextromethorphan: CNS depression; cyclosporin: immunosuppression).
  • For a prodrug, induction ↑ the therapeutic effect (slide 53).
  • Step 3 — the usual trap is the option in the opposite direction of the affected drug's own action (hyperglycemia for inhibited glyburide; hypertension belongs to tyramine/MAOI, not Coumadin).
